## Epperson v. Arkansas, 393 U.S. 97 (1968)

Read via the official US Reports text (Library of Congress digitized volume) — primary source, not a summary.

**Decision**: Argued 1968-10-16, decided **1968-11-12**. Unanimous in result (9-0, reversing the Arkansas Supreme Court), but not in reasoning — Justice **Abe Fortas** wrote the opinion of the Court; Justices Black, Harlan, and Stewart each wrote separate concurrences rather than joining the full reasoning. Black in particular doubted the case was "genuinely justiciable" since Arkansas had never once attempted to enforce the law.

**The statute**: Initiated Act No. 1, Ark. Acts 1929 (approved by voters 1928-11-06, Ark. Stat. Ann. §§80-1627, 80-1628). Made it unlawful for any teacher in a public school/university "to teach the theory or doctrine that mankind ascended or descended from a lower order of animals," or to use a textbook teaching that theory — a misdemeanor. **The Court's own opinion states directly**: "The Arkansas statute was an adaptation of the famous Tennessee 'monkey law' which that State adopted in 1925" (the Butler Act) — confirming the direct lineage from Tennessee. History: Rep. Astor L. Rotenberry introduced an anti-evolution bill Jan. 1927; passed the House 51-46, tabled twice in the Senate. Rotenberry then teamed with Ben Bogard (Little Rock Baptist pastor, founder of the American Anti-Evolution Association) to put it on the ballot as a popular initiative — passed Nov. 1928 with 63% of the vote.

**Susan Epperson**: Graduated from Arkansas's own school system, earned a master's in zoology (University of Illinois), hired by Little Rock schools fall 1964 to teach 10th-grade biology at Central High School. For 1965-66 the school adopted a new textbook with a chapter on human evolutionary origin — she was contractually required to teach it, exposing her to criminal liability and dismissal. She sued in Arkansas Chancery Court; a parent, H.H. Blanchard, intervened in support. Appellate counsel: Eugene R. Warren (argued), Bruce T. Bullion (brief). **The ACLU filed an amicus brief in support** (Leo Pfeffer, Melvin L. Wulf, Joseph B. Robison) — not her direct counsel of record, an amicus. Procedural path: Chancellor Murray O. Reed ruled the law unconstitutional; the Arkansas Supreme Court reversed in a two-sentence per curiam opinion (242 Ark. 922, 416 S.W.2d 322 (1967)) upholding it as a valid curriculum power, expressly declining the First Amendment question.

**Holding (Establishment Clause)**: "the law must be stricken because of its conflict with the constitutional prohibition of state laws respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof. The overriding fact is that Arkansas' law selects from the body of knowledge a particular segment which it proscribes for the sole reason that it is deemed to conflict with a particular religious doctrine; that is, with a particular interpretation of the Book of Genesis by a particular religious group." Further: "The law's effort was confined to an attempt to blot out a particular theory because of its supposed conflict with the Biblical account, literally read." On neutrality: "Government in our democracy, state and national, must be neutral in matters of religious theory, doctrine, and practice. It may not be hostile to any religion or to the advocacy of no-religion; and it may not aid, foster, or promote one religion or religious theory against another or even against the militant opposite. The First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion." Also quotes *Watson v. Jones*, 13 Wall. 679, 728 (1872): "The law knows no heresy, and is committed to the support of no dogma, the establishment of no sect."

**Practical fate / Mississippi**: Footnote 9: "no record of any prosecutions in Arkansas under its statute" in 40 years — "possibly more of a curiosity than a vital fact of life." Footnote 8 confirms: "Only Arkansas and Mississippi have such 'anti-evolution' or 'monkey' laws on their books" as of 1968 (Miss. Code Ann. §§6798, 6799 (1942)); notes Tennessee's law was repealed in 1967, and Oklahoma had passed and repealed an anti-evolution law by 1926. Mississippi's own law was separately struck down two years later: **Smith v. State, 242 So. 2d 692 (Miss. 1970)**, decided 1970-12-21 — the Mississippi Supreme Court held Epperson had "for all practical purposes already held that our anti-evolution statutes are unconstitutional."

## Edwards v. Aguillard, 482 U.S. 578 (1987)

Read via Cornell LII and FindLaw (both verbatim opinion reproductions, cross-checked against each other).

**Decision**: Decided **1987-06-19**. **7-2**. Justice **William Brennan** wrote the opinion, joined in full by Marshall, Blackmun, Powell, Stevens, and by O'Connor in all but Part II. Powell wrote a separate concurrence (joined by O'Connor); White concurred only in the judgment. **Scalia dissented, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist** (confirmed).

**The statute**: Louisiana's "Balanced Treatment for Creation-Science and Evolution-Science Act." Introduced by state Senator **Bill Keith** (Caddo Parish), June 1980; signed by Governor **David Treen**, 1981-07-20. Did not require either evolution or "creation science" be taught, but mandated that if one was taught, the other must be given "balanced treatment."

**Procedural history (genuinely different from Kitzmiller)**: Reached the Supreme Court via **summary judgment**, not a trial. District Court (E.D. La., Judge Adrian Duplantier) granted summary judgment to challengers 1985-01-10, finding no secular purpose (*Aguillard v. Treen*, 634 F. Supp. 426 (E.D. La. 1985)). Fifth Circuit affirmed 1985-07-08 (*Aguillard v. Edwards*, 765 F.2d 1251 (5th Cir. 1985)), finding the legislature's "actual intent was 'to discredit evolution by counterbalancing its teaching at every turn with the teaching of creationism, a religious belief,'" observing the Act "continues the battle William Jennings Bryan carried to his grave." The Supreme Court's decision rested on the pleadings and legislative history alone — no trial record, no witnesses, unlike Kitzmiller's six-week trial.

**Holding (Lemon test, purpose prong)**: "While the Court is normally deferential to a State's articulation of a secular purpose, it is required that the statement of such purpose be sincere and not a sham" (482 U.S. at 586-87). The Act "does not grant teachers a flexibility that they did not already possess" — no law had ever prohibited Louisiana teachers from teaching any scientific theory, undercutting the "academic freedom" rationale. Central holding: "The preeminent purpose of the Louisiana Legislature was clearly to advance the religious viewpoint that a supernatural being created humankind" (482 U.S. at 591); the Act was "designed either to promote the theory of creation science which embodies a particular religious tenet or to prohibit the teaching of a scientific theory disfavored by certain religious sects."

**Scalia's dissent**: Argued the majority was too quick to infer unconstitutional religious motive from a law stating a facially secular purpose; courts should be reluctant to second-guess a legislature's stated purpose absent strong evidence of insincerity. (Quotes below sourced via a non-official reproduction, flagged as needing one more cross-check before treating exact pincites as settled): accused the majority of resting on "its visceral knowledge regarding what *must* have motivated the legislators"; argued the Legislature had "explicitly set forth its secular purpose ('protecting academic freedom') in the very text of the Act"; invoked the Court's general "reluctance to attribute unconstitutional motives to the States"; framed "academic freedom" as students' "freedom from indoctrination."

**Strategic lineage to later "academic freedom" laws**: Confirmed via NCSE's own analysis and a 30-years-later retrospective (The Conversation). Edwards left open that a law could survive if it had a genuine secular purpose and didn't name religion or "creation science" explicitly — the 2000s-2010s "academic freedom act" template (the Discovery Institute's own model language, used in Louisiana's 2008 Louisiana Science Education Act and Tennessee's 2012 law) deliberately dropped "creation science" and religious language for "critical thinking"/"scientific strengths and weaknesses" phrasing. NCSE's Joshua Rosenau: these bills sound "innocuous" on their face but "they chose to question only areas that religious conservatives are uncomfortable with. There is a religious agenda here."

## Kitzmiller v. Dover's doctrinal link to both rulings

Confirmed directly (opinion read via CourtListener). Judge Jones relies on both repeatedly: cites Edwards as controlling precedent for applying the Lemon test to a creationism-adjacent curriculum mandate ("Edwards... held that a requirement that public schools teach 'creation science' along with evolution violated the Establishment Clause"); cites Epperson as the foundational case establishing that evolution-opposition curriculum law is historically religiously motivated, and situates it chronologically as predating Lemon (1971) and the later "endorsement test." Kitzmiller is explicitly an application of existing Supreme Court Establishment Clause precedent (Lemon, shaped by Edwards, traced to Epperson), not a standalone ruling.

## National organizations — founding facts

- **NCSE**: Founded by **Stanley L. Weinberg**, a retired Iowa high school biology teacher. In 1980 Weinberg began organizing state-level "Committees of Correspondence" tracking/countering creationist legislative efforts, linked by a Creation/Evolution Newsletter; formally incorporated as the National Center for Science Education in **1983** (1981 is commonly cited as the founding/organizing year — both years appear depending on whether "founded" means the organizing effort or legal incorporation). Headquartered in Oakland, CA.
- **ACLU**: Founded **1920** in New York (Roger Baldwin among the founders), emerging from the National Civil Liberties Bureau in response to the WWI-era Palmer Raids — unrelated to evolution at founding. The Scopes trial (1925) was one of its earliest and most famous cases: it recruited John Scopes to challenge Tennessee's Butler Act and partnered with Clarence Darrow for his defense.
- **Discovery Institute**: Founded **1991** (not 1990) by **Bruce Chapman** (a former Reagan-era official) and **George Gilder**, in Seattle, as a spinoff of the Hudson Institute. Its **Center for Science and Culture** (originally "Center for the Renewal of Science and Culture," the intelligent-design-focused unit) was established **1996**.
- **The Wedge Document**: Real, primary-source-adjacent, well-archived. Drafted 1998 by Center for the Renewal of Science and Culture staff; leaked online 1999-02-05 (posted by Tim Rhodes, who'd received it late January 1999). Discovery Institute co-founder/CSC Vice President Stephen C. Meyer later acknowledged the Institute as its source. NCSE hosts the full text. Its "Governing Goals": "To defeat scientific materialism and its destructive moral, cultural and political legacies" and "To replace materialistic explanations with the theistic understanding that nature and human beings are created by God." "Five Year Goals" (1999-2003) included seeing intelligent design "as an accepted alternative in the sciences." "Twenty Year Goals" aimed for intelligent design to become "the dominant perspective in science" across multiple disciplines.
