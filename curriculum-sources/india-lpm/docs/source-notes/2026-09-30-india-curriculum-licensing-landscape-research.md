## 1. Publishers — who actually publishes India's national curriculum documents

- **NCERT** (National Council of Educational Research and Training) prepares the National Curriculum Framework and publishes the standard national textbooks. Set up by the Government of India in 1961, but legally a **registered society** under the Societies Registration Act, 1860 (Act XXI) — not a government department itself, described as "autonomous" under the Ministry of Education. Sources: [Vajiram & Ravi](https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/ncert/), [understandupsc.com](https://www.understandupsc.com/national-council-of-educational-research-and-training-ncert/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Council_of_Educational_Research_and_Training).
- **CBSE** (Central Board of Secondary Education) sets curricula/syllabi for its affiliated schools and runs board exams. Also an **autonomous body registered under the Societies Registration Act, 1860**, under the Ministry of Education, not a government department itself. Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Board_of_Secondary_Education), [dsel.education.gov.in](https://dsel.education.gov.in/index.php/cbse).
- **NCF 2023** (National Curriculum Framework for School Education) was prepared by NCERT under NEP 2020's vision, covering ages 3–18. Sources: [NCF 2023 PDF (official)](https://ncf.ncert.gov.in/webadmin/assets/b27f04eb-65af-467f-af12-105275251546), [ncert.nic.in](https://ncert.nic.in/focus-group.php?ln=en).
- **Why this matters legally:** neither NCERT nor CBSE is literally "the Government or a department of the Government" — the literal wording of the Copyright Act's "government work" test (see §2). Both are government-controlled/funded autonomous societies. Whether their publications automatically count as statutory "government works" is genuinely disputed/unclear — one legal-scholarship source (Dhonchak, SpicyIP, §5) asserts NCERT and state textbook boards *do* qualify under Sections 2(k) and 17(dd), but no court ruling squarely settling this for NCERT/CBSE specifically was found.

## 2. Government-work copyright — what Indian copyright law actually says

Primary source: the Copyright Act 1957 ([copyright.gov.in PDF](https://copyright.gov.in/Documents/Copyright_Act_1957.pdf), [indiacode.nic.in bare act](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/15356/1/the_copyright_act,_1957.pdf) — both returned connection errors on direct fetch, so the quoted text below comes via [IndianKanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/) and [Wikimedia Commons' own summary](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/India), a secondary-source-of-primary-text limitation worth naming).

- **Section 2(k) — "government work"**: a work made or published by or under the direction or control of (i) the Government or any department of the Government, (ii) any Legislature in India, or (iii) any court/tribunal/judicial authority. Source: [IndianKanoon Sec 2(k)](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1273687/).
- **Section 17(d)**: for a Government work, Government is (absent contrary agreement) the first owner of copyright. **Section 17(dd)**: for a work made/first published by or under the control of a "public undertaking" (a body corporate established by or under a Central/Provincial/State Act, or a government company), that public undertaking is the first owner. Source: [IndianKanoon Sec 17](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1404402/).
- **Section 28 — term for government works**: **60 years from the beginning of the calendar year following first publication**. Section 28A gives the same 60-year term for "public undertaking" works. Sources: [IndianKanoon Sec 28](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/176237/), [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/India).
- **Section 52 fair-dealing exceptions**: standard categories (private/research use, criticism/review, news reporting, judicial/legislative proceedings, teacher/student reproduction for instruction), plus a narrow specific exception for legislature secretariat works — **not** a general exception for all government publications. Sources: [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/copyright/1184954/exception-to-infringement-of-copyright-section-52-of-the-copyright-act-1957), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright_law_of_India).
- **India does NOT have a blanket "government works = public domain" rule** the way, e.g., US federal government works are public domain by statute (17 U.S.C. §105). Indian government works are copyrighted, just with a fixed 60-year term instead of life+60.
- **The single most important open legal question found**: because NCERT and CBSE are registered societies rather than literal "government departments" or Act-created "public undertakings," it is not settled by plain statutory text whether Section 2(k)/17(d) (60-year term) or ordinary Section 22 (life-of-author+60, or 60 years from publication for anonymous/corporate works — a practically similar term but different ownership mechanics) governs their output. One scholarly source (Dhonchak, §5) treats NCERT/state boards as covered by 2(k)/17(dd) without deeply litigating the point; no source resolves it via case law. **Worth a real lawyer's read, not something resolved by search results.**

## 3. Open licensing — do NCERT/CBSE already publish anything openly?

**Split finding: some open-licensed supplementary material exists, but the core textbooks and curriculum/syllabus documents are explicitly all-rights-reserved.**

**Evidence for "all rights reserved":**
- NCERT's actual **state-adoption license template** (read directly, not summarized) states verbatim: *"The NCERT is the sole owner of copyright of the WORK,"* confines resale to the licensed state only, requires a 5% royalty + 12% GST, and states *"The Word 'Copyright' for the listed books... shall vest with the NCERT in perpetuity."* Source: [NCERT agreement PDF](https://ncert.nic.in/pdf/announcement/otherannouncements/teachersandresearchers/agreement-2019.pdf) (read in full).
- NCERT's own press release: *"unauthorised publishers have been printing NCERT school textbooks... without obtaining permission"* and unpermitted use "will face legal action under the Copyright Act 1957." Source: [schoolserv.in summary](https://www.schoolserv.in/NCERT-Warns-Against-Copyright-Infringement-of-Textbook-Content/); original [ncert.nic.in press release](https://www.ncert.nic.in/pdf/announcement/notices/Press_Release_Copyright_Infringement-NCERT.pdf) could not be loaded directly (connection errors) — relying on the secondary summary for exact wording.
- CBSE curriculum PDFs reportedly carry: *"This book or any part thereof may not be reproduced by any person or agency in any manner"* — found via search excerpt of cbseacademic.nic.in PDFs, **not independently confirmed by reading a PDF directly** — treat as likely-but-not-personally-verified.
- NCERT's terms-of-use language (found via search, direct load of [osre.ncert.gov.in/copyright-policy](https://osre.ncert.gov.in/copyright-policy) failed every attempt): *"contents of the NCERT website may not be reproduced partially or fully, without due permission,"* with "NCERT E-content" under a separate license, reproduction outside its terms risking "legal remedies for infringement of copyright and/or moral rights."

**Evidence for actual open licensing (narrower scope):**
- **NROER** (National Repository of Open Educational Resources), an NCERT/CIET project launched 2013-08-14, defaulted to **CC BY-SA**. Appears now inactive/superseded, no precise retirement date found. Sources: [Creative Commons 2013 announcement](https://creativecommons.org/2013/08/14/india-launches-national-repository-of-open-educational-resources/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Repository_of_Open_Educational_Resources).
- Search snippets (**not independently confirmed** — every direct attempt to load epathshala.nic.in or osre.ncert.gov.in's license pages failed with connection errors) claim NCERT textbooks on the **DIKSHA** platform are licensed **CC BY-NC-ND**, with other DIKSHA resources under **CC BY-NC-SA**. This sits in real tension with the all-rights-reserved language directly confirmed above. **A decision-maker should get someone to actually load `epathshala.nic.in/pages.php?id=license` and `osre.ncert.gov.in/copyright-policy` directly** (possibly just bot-blocked, not evidence of anything hidden) before relying on the NC-ND claim.
- No evidence found of **GODL-India** (Government Open Data License) being applied to NCERT/CBSE curriculum or textbook text specifically, despite GODL's own definition being broad enough to cover documents ([GODL text](https://punjab.data.gov.in/Godl), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Template:GODL-India)) — it is opt-in per dataset and no NCERT/CBSE curriculum listing under it was found on data.gov.in.

**Bottom line**: "free NCERT PDF downloads" are free-to-read distribution, not a redistribution license — NCERT has repeatedly and explicitly reserved republication rights in its own primary documents. Any actual CC license, where confirmed, applies to a narrower slice (NROER-era content, possibly some DIKSHA resources under terms not fully verified here) — not to the core textbook/curriculum-framework text as a whole.

## 4. State variation — does licensing vary meaningfully by state?

**Yes — real, concrete, documented variation, comparable in kind (not in underlying legal mechanism) to Germany's per-Land variation:**

- **Maharashtra (Balbharati)**: restrictive. Prohibits redistribution without a licensing agreement; a 2020-revised policy raised third-party publisher registration fees from ₹1,000 to ₹17,700 — a real, documented controversy (the Association of Publishers and Distributors publicly argued Balbharati shouldn't be able to copyright material that is "just compiled facts," and objected to a reported ₹63,000-per-subject-per-standard-per-medium fee). Sources: [Balbharati Copyright Policy PDF](https://services.ebalbharati.in/copyright/pdfs/Revised_Policy_26_Oct_2020.pdf), [Balbharati FAQ PDF](https://services.ebalbharati.in/copyright/pdfs/Balbharati_Licensing_FAQ.pdf), [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2021/07/copyright-ownership-in-state-board-textbooks-impediments-to-accessibility.html), [The Bridge Chronicle](https://www.thebridgechronicle.com/pune/balbharati-can%E2%80%99t-claim-copyright-over-textbooks-apd-19259).
- **Karnataka**: genuinely open. DSERT's Karnataka Open Educational Resources (KOER) licenses its textbooks/materials under **CC BY-SA 4.0**, explicitly more permissive than NCERT's own NROER (hosted "in full," unlike NROER's several missing chapters, per the SpicyIP review). Sources: [KOER portal](https://koer-en.karnatakaeducation.org.in/index.php/Main_Page), [KOER Text Books page](https://karnatakaeducation.org.in/KOER/en/index.php/Text_Books).
- **Tamil Nadu**: textbooks (Samacheer Kalvi) are hosted for free download, but **no specific, citable copyright/license policy statement was found** the way one was found for Maharashtra and Karnataka — a real gap, not an assumption of either openness or restriction.
- **Political/curriculum divergence, separate from copyright**: Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Maharashtra have also politically resisted parts of the central NCF/NEP 2020 (three-language formula, curriculum content); Karnataka has set up its own curriculum-design expert bodies. Education is a "Concurrent" subject under India's constitution, so states can legally diverge. Source: [ORF analysis](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/nep-national-curriculum-framework-and-the-federal-challenges-in-india).
- **Net assessment**: licensing status is NOT uniform across states. At minimum Maharashtra and Karnataka sit at opposite ends; Tamil Nadu's position is unconfirmed either way. A real redistribution-rights review would need to check each state board separately, the same way this ecosystem already checks per-Land for Germany.

## 5. Precedent — has anyone bulk-redistributed Indian government educational publications, and what happened?

- **Closest direct precedent (standards, not curriculum, but instructive)**: Carl Malamud (public.resource.org), with Sushant Sinha (Indian Kanoon) and Srinivas Kodali, republished ~19,000+ Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) technical standards for free starting 2013–2014. BIS sent legal/copyright objection notices; rather than complying, the trio filed a Public Interest Litigation in the Delhi High Court arguing a constitutional/RTI-based right to know legally-binding standards. Reported outcome: BIS voluntarily put ~14,000 of ~21,000 standards online for free over the following two years, without a definitive reported final court judgment found. Sources: [SpicyIP](https://spicyip.com/2017/02/copyright-in-standards-taking-a-look-at-the-pil-by-malamud-sinha-kodali-against-bis.html), [Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/article/1057344/how-an-american-activist-helped-make-indian-standards-available-for-free). Establishes that Indian government bodies do actively assert copyright over bulk-republished official documents, and that public-interest litigation plus public pressure has previously produced voluntary partial opening, without dispositive case law.
- **Directly on curriculum content**: GitHub repos already scrape/bulk-host NCERT textbook PDFs (e.g. [aayushdutt/ncert-downloader](https://github.com/aayushdutt/ncert-downloader), [avallark/NCERT-TextBooks](https://github.com/avallark/NCERT-TextBooks)), and a Hugging Face dataset collection ("NCERT_Dataset" by KadamParth) hosts NCERT textbook text as ML training data, ~120k items across 31 datasets as of March 2025, **with no license or NCERT-permission statement shown**. Source: [HuggingFace collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/KadamParth/ncert-dataset-67a254fe451f1bf2efd342fb). No evidence found of NCERT sending takedown notices against these specific repos/datasets — but silence is not evidence of permission, and no reported controversy/lawsuit was found either way.
- **Best scholarly treatment found**: Anupriya Dhonchak & Rahul Bajaj, "Government Copyright in School Textbooks and the Fundamental Right to Education," *Indian Journal of Constitutional Law*, Vol. 10 (2021), NALSAR — only accessible via search-engine summary here (direct PDF fetch to [ijcl.nalsar.ac.in](https://ijcl.nalsar.ac.in/storage/2022/02/DhonchakBajaj_IJCL_volume10_2021.pdf) returned server errors every attempt); reportedly argues restrictive government copyright over textbooks impedes the constitutional right to education. Companion piece read directly: [SpicyIP, "Copyright Ownership in State Board Textbooks: Impediments to Accessibility," July 2021](https://spicyip.com/2021/07/copyright-ownership-in-state-board-textbooks-impediments-to-accessibility.html) — covers the Balbharati fee increases, the Karnataka contrast, NCERT's incomplete NROER hosting, and cites 1949 Constituent Assembly debate history (R.R. Diwakar) suggesting government textbook publishing was originally meant as cost-recovery, not revenue/IP-control-driven. **Recommend getting the full IJCL PDF directly** before relying on the search summary of its detailed argument.

## Gaps flagged — do not treat as settled

1. Whether NCERT/CBSE's registered-society legal form actually brings them inside the Section 2(k)/17(d) "government work" definition, vs. ordinary corporate/anonymous-work copyright rules — genuinely unresolved in what was found.
2. The exact, current license terms on NCERT's own epathshala/osre.ncert.gov.in pages — automated fetches to these specific .gov.in pages failed every time, so the "CC BY-NC-ND textbooks" claim rests only on search-engine snippets and conflicts with the all-rights-reserved language directly confirmed elsewhere. The single most important thing for a human (or a session with working access to those pages) to verify directly before relying on it.
3. Tamil Nadu's specific copyright/licensing policy for its state textbooks/curriculum — not found.
4. Whether the Malamud/BIS PIL ever reached a final court judgment, versus being resolved by voluntary partial disclosure — sources describe the voluntary opening but not a dispositive ruling.
5. No IJCL PDF full-text read (server errors); relying on search-summary only for that source's detailed legal argument.

No legal conclusion or course of action is recommended here, by design — this is a research hand-off for a real lawyer's or decision-maker's read.
